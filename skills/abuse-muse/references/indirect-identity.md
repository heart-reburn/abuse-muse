# Indirect identity

Yellow means Meta could guess or indirectly understand who the user is, even when no name, address, or account number is sent.

## What yellow covers

- Demographic clues, such as gender, age range, and language or dialect.

- Work clues, such as job title, industry, employer size, work schedule, and professional tools.

- Place clues, such as city, region, commute, school, gym, or frequent place, even without a street address.

- Life clues, such as family composition, routine, hobbies, health hints, income range, and spending habits.

- History clues, such as education, past jobs, courses taken, and projects worked on.

- Style and timing clues, such as writing style, distinctive phrasing, a query sent immediately after a real event, and the order of queries in a session, even when no fact in a single query is identifying.

- A folder, vault, or set of notes that together reveal those clues, even when each single note looks harmless.

## Why a combination matters

- One broad clue on its own may only narrow a population to millions of people.

- Two or three clues together can narrow that population to a small group, a workplace, or a single person.

- Clues accumulate across sessions, because Muse can remember interests, jobs, places, and routines from earlier prompts.

- A unique combination is the risk, not just a single sensitive word.

## Examples

- A user who is female, age 20 to 30, an accountant in New York who likes hiking, is much easier to narrow than any one of those clues alone.

- A user who mentions a rare hobby, a specific course, and a specific work schedule in the same prompt gives a fingerprint, even with no name.

- A user who shares a daily routine, a commute, and a workplace type gives a pattern that can be matched to a real person.

## Generalization ladder

- Replace an exact age with a broad adult range, or remove age when it does not change the task.

- Replace a specific job title with a broad role, such as professional, student, or creator.

- Replace a city with a region, and replace a region with a country when the task allows it.

- Replace a specific employer, school, product, or client with a generic type.

- Replace a specific routine with a generic pattern, such as daytime work, without times and places.

- Split a task across prompts only when each prompt on its own stays broad, and do not rebuild the full profile in one session.

## Yellow action

- Name the indirect clues found, and show the generalized version before sending.

- Ask the user whether the generalized version still solves the task.

- Block the prompt when generalization would lose the task and the combination is uniquely identifying.

## Remember what was already revealed

Keep a local, type-only record of clue types already sent, and check each new draft against it. See `revealed-clues.md`.

## Compartmentalization

- Public research can reveal identity, even when no direct identifier is sent, because a string of queries about a rare condition, a niche job, a specific city, and a specific school can fingerprint a user.

- Use different tools or sessions for different domains, and keep work, health, location, and personal research separate. Do not cross-link them in one account, one chat, or one invented persona.

- Use generic queries first, and do not chain clues that together identify the user.

- Do the work locally first, and send only the minimum that the task actually needs. Do not paste a whole document when a short, paraphrased question will do, and do not paste your own writing verbatim when a generic paraphrase will do, because writing style itself is a clue.

- Do not ask about something immediately after the real event it relates to, if timing alone would link the query to you. Wait, generalize, or do it locally.

- Assume a clue that looks broad today may become identifying later, as more data is correlated and as identification tools improve. That is why a clue should be shared only if it is needed now, not just because it seems safe now.

- If a task does need personal context, do it locally, or wait for a more private environment, rather than pasting it into Muse.

- A new session on the same machine is weak separation. For a sensitive domain, use a separate machine, and don't carry files, accounts, trackers, or mappings between it and your everyday machine.

- Delete local originals and mappings when they're no longer needed, and don't keep them in the same place you send from.
